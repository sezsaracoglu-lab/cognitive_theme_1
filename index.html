🧵 Synthesis

One thesis for the whole theme: attention is a limited, selective resource, and awareness depends on it. Part A (Simons) shows the cost: when attention is focused, fully visible but unexpected objects and sounds go unnoticed (the umbrella woman, the gorilla, ‘I am a gorilla’), more so when the task is demanding, when the object is dissimilar to what we attend, and even when the load is a memory task. And we don’t know what we miss. Part B (Sternberg) asks how selection works: we pick one voice by its physical cues, notice physical but not semantic changes in an ignored ear, yet sometimes catch our own name. Filter and bottleneck theories differ in where selection happens: early (Broadbent), selective (Moray), attenuated (Treisman), late (Deutsch & Deutsch), or in two stages (Neisser).

How the pieces connect: dichotic listening is in both readings · inattentional deafness (A) = the cost of solving the cocktail party problem (B) · Fougnie & Marois (A) = central capacity, the bottleneck of B · own name (B) shows selection is not absolute, just as some people do notice the gorilla (A).

🔦 The limits of awareness

Our perception is limited by our minds, not our eyes

  • More than 50 years of research: perception of the world is limited ‘not by our eyes and ears, but by our minds’.
  • We appear able to process only one stream of information at a time, filtering other information from awareness.
  • To a large extent we perceive only what receives the focus of our cognitive effort: our attention.
  • Most people believe they notice editing errors, multitask well and are fully aware of their surroundings, and they are most likely wrong.
  • Some researchers argue there is no real multitasking, only rapid switching between tasks.

🎧 Dichotic listening and cognitive deafness

Deaf to the substance of the ignored speech

  • Dichotic listening: different speech streams to each ear; repeat (shadow) the left-ear message as quickly and accurately as possible.
  • You don’t notice the right-ear speaker switching language, being replaced by a similar voice, or turning into nonsense: you are ‘deaf to the substance’.
  • This is not a sensory limit but cognitive deafness, due to focused, selective attention.
  • Your own name in the ignored ear is noticed only about one-third of the time (Conway, Cowan & Bunting, 2001), perhaps only because some attention still goes to that stream (Holender, 1986).
  • Large physical changes (male → female voice) are noticed, but not substantive ones, except rarely.
🪝 Memory hook: One ear works 🎧, the other ear is decoration.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Selective attention filters the meaning of unattended speech, leaving only coarse physical features.
Tap to open ▸
With two different messages in your ears and the job of repeating one, you completely miss the meaning of the other. You’d notice if the voice changed from a man to a woman, but not if it switched to German.

🎞️ Selective looking: a visual dichotic task

Neisser & Becklen (1975)

  • The same pattern holds for vision: Neisser & Becklen (1975) devised a visual analogue of dichotic listening.
  • Videos of two distinct, partly transparent, overlapping events (a hand-clapping game and ball passing).
  • Both events produced signals on the retina regardless of which one was attended.
  • Counting actions in one event, participants often missed unexpected events in the other (clappers stopping to shake hands).
  • They noticed ‘other stuff’ was happening but not its meaning: unaware even when looking right at it.
🪝 Memory hook: Two films on one screen 🎞️🎞️: you watch one, the other becomes wallpaper.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Selective visual attention, like selective listening, filters the meaning of unattended events even when they fall on the retina.
Tap to open ▸
If two videos are layered on top of each other and you count claps in one, you can miss people shaking hands in the other, even though it’s right in front of your eyes.

🦍 The umbrella woman and the invisible gorilla

Neisser (1979); Simons & Chabris (1999)

  • Neisser (1979): count passes by the white team in superimposed videos; a woman with an umbrella strolled through and was often missed.
  • Dismissed for about 20 years because the displays looked ‘ghostly’: surely we’d notice in an opaque, vivid scene.
  • Simons & Chabris (1999) replicated it and added a version filmed with one camera, fully visible players moving around each other.
  • A woman in a gorilla suit walked in, faced the camera, thumped her chest and left after 9 s: fully half of observers counting white passes missed it.
  • Inattentional blindness: failure to notice an unexpected object or event when attention is focused elsewhere (Mack & Rock, 1998).
🪝 Memory hook: Half the room misses a chest-thumping gorilla 🦍 while counting passes 🏀.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Even fully visible, salient events go unnoticed when attention is engaged elsewhere: inattentional blindness.
Tap to open ▸
People counting basketball passes were so focused that half of them didn’t see a person in a gorilla suit walk right through the middle of the game and thump her chest.

🎯 What determines whether we notice

Similarity, effort and memory load

  • Similarity: unexpected objects sharing features with attended items are noticed more (Most et al., 2001); counting black-team passes → gorilla noticed more.
  • But even unique items go unnoticed: ~30% missed a bright red cross for 5 s while monitoring black shapes (Most et al., 2001).
  • Effort: separate counts of bounce and aerial passes, or tracking faster objects (Simons & Jensen, 2009), reduce noticing.
  • Memory load: people miss unexpected visual objects while doing a memory task (Fougnie & Marois, 2007) → limits are not purely visual but reflect attentional capacity.
  • Summary: more attention demand, less similarity to attended items and more distraction → less noticing: ‘tunnel vision’.
🪝 Memory hook: Busy 💪, dissimilar 🎨, distracted 🧠 = blind.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Noticing depends on spare attentional capacity and on how similar the unexpected object is to what you’re attending.
Tap to open ▸
You’re more likely to miss the gorilla if your task is hard, if the gorilla doesn’t look like what you’re watching, and if your mind is busy with something else, even a memory task.

🚦 Inattentional blindness and deafness in the real world

Fight club, unicycling clowns and ‘I am a gorilla’

  • Chabris et al. (2011) simulated a Boston police case: joggers following an experimenter ran past a staged fight; 65% missed it at night, 44% in daylight.
  • This lends plausibility to the officer’s claim that he never saw the beating.
  • Auditory distraction causes failures to see: phoning while driving or walking reduces situation awareness (Strayer & Johnston, 2001).
  • Hyman et al.: pedestrians talking on a phone were less likely to notice a unicycling clown crossing their path.
  • Inattentional deafness: listeners missed a voice repeating ‘I am a gorilla’ (Dalton & Fraenkel, 2012); visual load causes deafness too (Macdonald & Lavie, 2011).
🪝 Memory hook: Phone in hand 📱 = clown unseen 🤡.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Inattentional blindness and deafness occur in natural settings, especially when attention is captured by other tasks such as phone calls.

👥 Individual differences in noticing

Working memory capacity and tracking ability

  • Do some people consistently notice the unexpected, or is everyone subject to structural limits? Still controversial.
  • Some studies: higher working memory capacity → more noticing (Hannon & Richards, 2010; Richards et al., 2010): more resources available.
  • Others: no relationship (Seegmiller, Watson & Strayer, 2011; Bredemeier & Simons, 2012).
  • Theory predicts both: more resources → notice more (Macdonald & Lavie, 2011), but high-WMC people also keep focus better → notice less.
  • Simons & Jensen (2009): tracking ability (some > 2× faster at 75% accuracy) was unrelated to noticing; trying to do the task is what matters.
🪝 Memory hook: Bigger engine 🚗, same tunnel vision.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Individual differences in noticing are inconsistent; task engagement, not task skill, predicts missing the unexpected.

🤔 Why inattentional blindness is counterintuitive

We don’t know what we’re missing

  • Nearly 90% believe they would spot the gorilla (Levin & Angelone, 2008); 78% agreed ‘people generally notice when something unexpected enters their field of view’ (Simons & Chabris, 2010).
  • People are also convinced they would spot movie errors or changes to a conversation partner (Levin & Angelone, 2008).
  • We think we see and remember far more of our surroundings than we do.
  • Explanation: our experiences mislead us: we’re aware of what we notice but unaware of what we miss.
  • We rarely confront proof of an obvious miss, so we assume our experience is representative and conclude that we notice unexpected events.
🪝 Memory hook: You can’t remember what you never saw 🙈, so you think you saw everything.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Our confidence in noticing comes from never experiencing our misses; inattentional blindness is hidden from the person who has it.
Tap to open ▸
Almost everyone is sure they’d see the gorilla. That’s because in daily life you never find out about all the things you missed, so it feels like you see it all.

🛡️ Why inattentional blindness exists and how to live with it

Evolution, modern risks and practical advice

  • Why weren’t our ancestors eaten? Intense focus may have been more useful than noticing rare unexpected events; most events don’t need immediate attention; others might notice and warn us.
  • Modern life: more distraction and higher speeds; a small delay can separate a fender-bender from a lethal collision.
  • Drivers keep phoning because they don’t notice how distracted they are (Strayer & Johnston, 2001): the same mistaken intuition.
  • No ‘magic elixir’ lets us notice everything, and that ‘would not be a good outcome anyway’.
  • What to do: avoid distractions (phone out of reach), and pay attention to what others might not notice: ‘looking is not the same as seeing’.
🪝 Memory hook: Focus 🔦 is a superpower with a blind spot.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Inattentional blindness is a by-product of useful focus; we can’t eliminate it but can reduce its consequences.

🔎 Search: feature, guided and conjunction

Attention as a search function

  • Attention has several functions; Sternberg discusses signal detection, search and selective attention.
  • Feature search: the target differs by one feature and pops out. Guided search: a feature narrows the candidates. Conjunction search: the target is defined by a combination of features (hardest).
  • In Figure 4.7 participants look for the upright black L among different distractors.
  • Madden et al. (2002), PET: conjunction search activated dorsal and ventral visual pathways and prefrontal cortex in young and older adults.
  • Harder search raised occipito-temporal activity in young adults; older adults showed high activity even in easy search (compensation) but recruited no regions outside visual pathways.
🪝 Memory hook: One odd feature pops out 🔴; a combination needs a careful scan 🔍.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Search difficulty depends on whether the target pops out by a single feature or must be found by combining features.

🍸 Selective attention and the cocktail party problem

Cherry’s shadowing studies

  • Selective attention: attending to one source while ignoring others, as at a dinner party with a vacuum-cleaner salesman on one side and juicy gossip on the other.
  • Cherry (1953): the cocktail party problem: tracking one conversation despite other conversations.
  • Shadowing with dichotic presentation: a different message to each ear; repeat one message immediately.
  • Participants shadowed well (with concentration) and noticed physical changes in the unattended ear (a tone, male → female voice).
  • They did not notice semantic changes: a switch from English to German, or speech played backwards.
🪝 Memory hook: The unattended ear hears the voice 🔊, not the words 📖.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Without attention, only physical features of a message are processed; meaning is lost.
Tap to open ▸
When you shadow one ear, you can still tell if the other ear switches from a man to a woman, but not if it switches to German or is played backwards. Attention passes physical features, not meaning.

📛 Hearing your own name

Who notices it, and how we select a voice

  • About one-third of people switch attention to their name when it is presented in the unattended ear.
  • Those who hear it tend to have limited working-memory capacity and are easily distracted (Conway, Cowan & Bunting, 2001); infants also shift attention to their name (Newman, 2005).
  • In a noisy restaurant three cues help: distinctive sensory features (pitch, pacing, rhythm), intensity, and location (Brungard & Simpson, 2007).
  • Attending to physical voice properties avoids distraction by the meaning of other messages; turning one ear toward the speaker uses the volume difference.
  • Recent studies: spatial cues matter less than how harmonious and rhythmic the target sounds (Darwin, 2008; Muente et al., 2010).
🪝 Memory hook: Your name is a password 🔑 that sometimes opens the filter.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Personally important information can break through unattended channels, especially for people with weaker attentional control.

🍾 Filter and bottleneck theories

What blocks, what slows, and where

  • The theories of selective attention belong to the group of filter and bottleneck theories; note how each theory reacted to the previous one (dialectic).
  • A filter blocks some information and lets only part of it pass to the next stage.
  • A bottleneck slows information down: only a limited amount gets through at a time.
  • The models differ in (1) whether they have a distinct filter, and (2) where it sits: early or late in processing.
  • Proponents of early and late filters both propose a bottleneck through which only a single source passes; they differ only in its position.
🪝 Memory hook: Filter = a sieve 🧹 that blocks; bottleneck = a narrow neck 🍾 that slows.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Attention theories differ in whether information is blocked (filter) or merely limited (bottleneck) and in how early selection happens.
Tap to open ▸
A filter decides what gets in and throws the rest away. A bottleneck lets things through, but only a few at a time. Attention theories argue about whether there’s a filter and how early it acts.

🚧 Broadbent’s early filter model

Selection right after the sensory level

  • One of the earliest theories (Broadbent, 1958): we filter information right after we notice it at the sensory level.
  • Multiple channels reach an attentional filter; channels are distinguished by physical characteristics (loudness, pitch, accent).
  • The filter lets only one channel proceed to perception, where meaning is assigned; other stimuli never reach perception.
  • Supported by Cherry: sensory changes in the unattended ear can be noticed (tone), but changes needing higher perceptual processing (English → German) are not.

The finding is important because it tells us where the limit lies. Fougnie and Marois (2007) showed that people also miss unexpected visual objects when their resources are taken up by a memory task. Therefore, as Simons concludes, ‘the limits are not purely visual. Instead, they appear to reflect limits on the capacity of attention.’ Inattentional blindness reflects a central, general-purpose attentional resource shared across tasks and modalities.

This has practical consequences: any cognitively demanding activity, such as mentally rehearsing something, solving a problem or holding a phone conversation, can make us blind to what is in front of us. That explains why cell-phone users miss a unicycling clown (Hyman et al., 2011) and why phoning while driving is risky even when it is hands-free (Strayer & Johnston, 2001). It also links IB to working memory, which motivates the research on working-memory capacity and noticing.

How does the inattentional deafness study differ from dichotic listening?

❓ Canvas question B1, word for word
B1

🩺 Triage: how to recognise and attack it
Symptom‘What is the difference?’: compare two paradigms.
DiagnosisDichotic listening/shadowing (Cherry; Moray; Treisman) vs inattentional deafness (Dalton & Fraenkel, 2012).
TreatmentDescribe each set-up → contrast: unexpected vs expected, channel vs event, task demand, what is measured → shared conclusion.
  • Dichotic listening: a different message to each ear; shadow one, ignore the other.
  • The ignored message is known and continuous; the question is what gets through (physical changes, own name).
  • Inattentional deafness: spatial conversations over headphones; an unexpected voice saying ‘I am a gorilla’ walks through.
  • No shadowing; a more natural scene; the critical event is a surprise, like the visual gorilla.

🪝 Memory hook: Two ears, two messages 🎧 vs one surprise voice 🦍🔊.
★ KEY INSIGHT · In dichotic listening you know there’s a second message and you try to ignore it. In inattentional deafness, a surprise voice walks through the scene and most people simply don’t hear it, the sound version of the gorilla.
Tap to open ▸

In dichotic listening (Cherry, 1953; Moray, 1959; Treisman, 1960), participants hear two different messages, one in each ear, and must shadow (repeat aloud) the message in one ear while ignoring the other. The unattended message is a known, continuous channel, and researchers measure which of its properties are noticed: physical changes (male to female voice, a tone) are noticed, semantic changes (switch to German, backward speech) are not, and one’s own name is noticed about one-third of the time (Moray, 1959; Conway et al., 2001).

In the inattentional deafness experiment (Dalton & Fraenkel, 2012), participants listen to a scene of spatially localized conversations and attend to one of them, without shadowing. During the scene an unexpected voice walks through repeatedly saying ‘I am a gorilla’, which many listeners fail to notice. Thus the key differences are: (1) unexpected event vs expected competing channel; (2) a single critical event in a natural scene vs a continuous message; (3) no shadowing demand; and (4) the question asked (‘did you notice anything unusual?’ vs ‘what did the other ear contain?’). It is the auditory analogue of the gorilla study. Both show that unattended information is processed far less than we expect: a cognitive, not a sensory, deafness.

  • Not naming the expected vs unexpected distinction.

The cocktail party problem refers to our ability to follow one conversation in a noisy environment with many speakers (Cherry, 1953). Which attention theories can explain hearing your own name?

❓ Canvas question B3, word for word
B3
Which theories of attention can explain the observation that you hear your name in an unattended message, and which ones cannot?
🩺 Triage: how to recognise and attack it
Symptom‘Which can and which cannot?’: sort theories by one test.
DiagnosisBroadbent, Moray, Treisman, Deutsch & Deutsch/Norman, Neisser.
TreatmentName the requirement (meaning of unattended input must be analysed) → check each theory → conclude.
  • Recognising your name requires meaning from the unattended ear.
  • Cannot: Broadbent’s early filter: only one channel passes; the unattended one is blocked before meaning.
  • Can: Moray’s selective filter (salient messages burst through), Treisman’s attenuation (weakened, but own name has a low threshold).
  • Can: Deutsch & Deutsch / Norman late filter (all input analysed for meaning first).
  • Can: Neisser’s synthesis (preattentive processing can trigger attentive capture).
🪝 Memory hook: Early block ✗ · weaken or late ✓.
★ KEY INSIGHT · To notice your name, your brain must have understood a word in the message you were ignoring. Broadbent says the ignored message is blocked before meaning, so he can’t explain it. The others let at least some meaning through.
Tap to open ▸

Hearing one’s own name in the unattended ear (Moray, 1959; ~one-third of people, Conway et al., 2001) shows that some meaning of the unattended message is analysed. Theories must therefore allow meaning-level processing of unattended input.

Cannot explain it: Broadbent’s (1958) early filter model. The filter selects one channel right after the sensory register on the basis of physical features; unattended information is blocked before perceptual/semantic processing, so a name (a meaningful item) should never be recognised.

Can explain it: (1) Moray’s selective filter, which blocks most information but lets very salient messages, such as one’s name, burst through; (2) Treisman’s attenuation model: unattended messages are weakened, not blocked, and words with a low recognition threshold, like one’s own name, are still activated by the weak signal; (3) the late-filter model of Deutsch and Deutsch (1963; Norman, 1968): all input is analysed for physical properties and meaning before selection, so a name can be recognised and selected; (4) Neisser’s synthesis: preattentive processing of the unattended stream can trigger attentive processing of relevant items. (A further nuance: people with lower working-memory capacity notice their name more often, which suggests attention lapses toward the unattended ear also play a role, Conway et al., 2001.)

  • Saying Treisman’s filter blocks unattended messages (it weakens them).

❓ Canvas question B4, word for word
B4
Is there a difference between filter theories and bottleneck theories of attention?
🩺 Triage: how to recognise and attack it
Symptom‘Is there a difference?’: give the definitional difference AND the nuance.
DiagnosisSternberg’s definitions of filter and bottleneck; early and late models.
TreatmentDefine both → note that the classic models are both → conclude with ‘yes in principle, overlapping in practice’.
  • Filter: blocks some information and passes only a part to the next stage.
  • Bottleneck: slows information down; a narrow passage where only one source passes at a time.
  • So yes, in principle: blocking vs slowing/limiting flow.
  • But Sternberg: early and late filter models both assume a bottleneck; they differ only in where it sits.
  • Treisman’s attenuator weakens rather than blocks: in between.
🪝 Memory hook: Filter = door 🚪 · bottleneck = narrow neck 🍾.
★ KEY INSIGHT · A filter is like a bouncer who sends some guests home. A bottleneck is a narrow door where everyone has to queue. Most attention theories have both: one narrow door, and the debate is where it is.
Tap to open ▸

In principle, yes. Sternberg defines a filter as a mechanism that blocks some of the information and thereby selects only part of the total information to pass to the next stage. A bottleneck slows down information passing through: it is a point of limited capacity where only one source of information can pass at a time, so other information is delayed rather than necessarily removed.

In practice, they overlap. The theories discussed in Sternberg belong to one group of ‘filter and bottleneck theories’. Both early-filter (Broadbent, 1958) and late-filter (Deutsch & Deutsch, 1963; Norman, 1968) models propose an attentional bottleneck through which only a single source of information can pass; the models differ only in where the bottleneck/filter is positioned (before or after semantic analysis). Treisman’s attenuation model blurs the distinction further, since its filter weakens rather than blocks. So the difference is mainly one of emphasis: blocking (what gets through) versus limited throughput (how much and how fast).

How do inattentional blindness and selective listening differ from task switching?

❓ Canvas question B5, word for word
B5
How are the phenomena studied in problems 1A and 1B different from task switching, which was introduced in the first lecture?
🩺 Triage: how to recognise and attack it
Symptom‘How are they different?’: contrast phenomena from the readings with a lecture concept.
DiagnosisSelective attention/IB/dichotic listening vs task switching (executive control, switch costs).
TreatmentDefine what 1A/1B study → define task switching → contrast on what is selected, timing and measurement.
  • 1A/1B: selective attention: choosing which input (stream, object) is processed at one moment.
  • Measured by awareness: noticing the gorilla, the other ear, your name.
  • Task switching: changing which task set (rule, goal) is active from one moment to the next.
  • Measured by switch costs: slower responses and more errors after a switch.
  • Link: what feels like multitasking is often rapid switching, with costs.
🪝 Memory hook: Which INPUT? 🔦 vs which TASK? 🔀.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The gorilla and the dichotic listening studies are about which information gets into your head while you do one task. Task switching is about jumping between two different tasks, and the time it costs your brain to change rules.
Tap to open ▸

The phenomena in problems 1A and 1B concern selective attention: how the system chooses which input (a voice, an ear, the white team’s passes) is processed while other simultaneously present input is filtered or attenuated. Their dependent measure is awareness/report of the unattended information (did you notice the gorilla, the language change, your name?), and the theories (Broadbent, Treisman, Deutsch & Deutsch) concern where in the information flow selection takes place.

Task switching concerns executive control over which task set (goal and rules) is active. Participants alternate between tasks (e.g. classify digits as odd/even, then as high/low), and the key finding is a switch cost: slower and less accurate responses on switch trials than on repeat trials, because the cognitive system must reconfigure the task set and overcome interference from the previous one. Thus, selective attention is about selecting among stimuli at the same time, whereas task switching is about selecting among tasks over time, measured by performance costs rather than awareness. They are related: everyday ‘multitasking’ (e.g. phoning while driving) often involves rapid switching, and the attentional limits that cause inattentional blindness are also why switching is costly.

  • Defining task switching as dividing attention between two simultaneous inputs.
  • Not naming the different dependent measures (awareness vs switch costs).

The Invisible Gorilla: What Makes Us Miss Things

❓ This lecture answers: How often do people miss a fully visible unexpected object, and what makes it more likely?
A.2.1
From ghostly videos to a gorilla

👻 Dismissed for ~20 years as “ghostly”. Simons & Chabris (1999) filmed it opaque with one camera: fully half missed a gorilla. Inattentional blindness: missing a fully visible unexpected object (Mack & Rock, 1998).

A.2.2
Factor 1: similarity to what you attend to

🎨 Objects that share features with attended items are noticed more: count black passes → see the black gorilla. Yet ≈30% missed a unique red cross while tracking black shapes (Most et al., 2001).

A.2.3
Factor 2: effort and memory load

💪 Bounce + aerial counts, or faster tracking (Simons & Jensen, 2009) → more missing. A memory task alone is enough (Fougnie & Marois, 2007): the limit is the capacity of attention, not vision.

❓ This lecture answers: Is this just a laboratory curiosity, or does it happen on streets, campuses and in the ears?
A.3.1
A fight nobody saw

A.3.2

🤔A.4

Individual Differences, False Intuitions and What to Do

❓ This lecture answers: Do some people notice more, why do the results surprise us, and what can we do?
A.4.1
Do some people notice more?

⚖️ Controversial. Higher working memory → more noticing (Hannon & Richards, 2010) or no link (Seegmiller et al., 2011; Bredemeier & Simons, 2012). More spare capacity, but also better staying focused.

A.4.2
Why the results surprise us

😲 ≈90% think they’d spot the gorilla (Levin & Angelone, 2008); 78% agree people notice the unexpected (Simons & Chabris, 2010). Our experiences mislead us: we’re unaware of what we missed.

A.4.3
Why did we survive, and why does it matter now?

🧬 Focus was useful; unexpected events are rare and rarely urgent; others may notice. Today’s speed and distraction raise the cost, yet drivers keep phoning (Strayer & Johnston, 2001).

A.4.4
What can you do?

🛠️ “Not much”, but reduce the consequences: phone out of reach while driving, and don’t assume others see you. Looking is not the same as seeing.

🍸B.1

Selective Attention and the Cocktail Party Problem

❓ This lecture answers: How do we follow one conversation among many, and what gets through from the ignored one?
B.1.1
Where the excerpt starts

🔎 End of visual search: feature, guided, conjunction search (Madden et al., 2002): brain volume down ~2% per decade; older adults activate visual areas more, apparently to compensate. Context only.

B.1.2
The cocktail party problem

🍸 Salesman on your right, gossip on your left. Cocktail party problem (Cherry, 1953; Bee & Micheyl, 2008): tracking one conversation among others, studied by shadowing with dichotic presentation.

B.1.3
What gets through from the unattended ear

✅ Physical changes (tone, male → female). ❌ Semantic ones (German, backwards). Own name ≈ ⅓, linked to limited working memory (Conway et al., 2001); infants too (Newman, 2005).

B.1.4
Three cues for following one voice

🎵 Voice characteristics, loudness, location (Brungard & Simpson, 2007); the difference between ears locates the source. But harmony and rhythm beat spatial cues (Darwin, 2008; Muente et al., 2010).

🚰B.2

Where Is the Filter? Theories of Selective Attention

❓ This lecture answers: Do we filter unattended information early, weaken it, or filter it late, after meaning?
B.2.1
Filters and bottlenecks

🚰 Theories grew dialectically. Filter: blocks some, passes part. Bottleneck: slows information. Is there a filter, and is it early or late?

B.2.2
Broadbent: an early filter

🧱 Broadbent (1958): filter at the sensory level; one channel reaches perception. Fits Cherry: a tone is noticed, German isn’t.

B.2.3
Moray: important messages break through

📛 Moray (1959): own name recognised (Wood & Cowan, 1995); against Broadbent (Gray & Wedderburn, 1960). Important messages burst through (Koivisto & Revonsuo, 2007; Marsh et al., 2007).

B.2.4
Treisman: attenuation

🔉 Ear-switch: first few words followed (1960); identical messages and bilingual translations noticed (1964). Filter weakens rather than blocks; meaning and relevance still analysed.

B.2.5
Deutsch and Deutsch: a late filter

🧠 Deutsch & Deutsch (1963; Norman, 1968): filter after physical and meaning analysis. Early and late both have one bottleneck; only its position differs.

B.2.6
Neisser’s synthesis

🧩 Neisser (1967): preattentive (automatic, parallel, physical only) + attentive (serial, uses working memory, relations, builds an object).
